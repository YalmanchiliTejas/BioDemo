Abstract

Lyophilization, or freeze-drying, is a widespread process for stabilizing (bio)pharmaceutical products. The primary drying phase is resource-intensive, making its optimization a focal point for improving economic and environmental sustainability. Mechanistic modeling of the primary drying phase has emerged as a key tool for designing robust processes, identifying optimal critical process parameters, and minimizing failure modes such as product collapse, uncontrolled pressure increase, or incomplete sublimation. This study extended the traditional modeling frameworks by incorporating an uncertainty analysis to account for process variability originating from process parameters and material attributes. Model validation and verification activities were performed to establish model credibility. The ensuing risk assessment quantified the failure mode probabilities. This methodology was capable of ensuring process robustness at a pilot scale freeze-dryer by construction of a regulatory compliant design space and the establishment of proven acceptable ranges. An iterative approach to collect process knowledge was conducted by updating the dried layer resistance estimates while verifying the design space. The transfer of the normal operating ranges to a commercial scale freeze-dryer was assisted by the model and was experimentally verified. Experimental results confirmed the model’s capability to predict process parameter combinations yielding acceptable critical quality attributes while minimizing process time. The model provides a scientific rationale for process parameters selection and can support in the process optimization, robustness assessment and process transfer activities necessary for a regulatory submission.

Introduction

Lyophilization, or freeze-drying, is commonly used to increase the shelf life of (bio)pharmaceuticals. During lyophilization, the product is first frozen, and the ice crystals are subsequently removed by sublimation during the primary drying phase. The removal of ice occurs at low temperature and high vacuum conditions during primary drying, making this process particularly suitable for heat-sensitive molecules. The desorption of the unfrozen water molecules takes place during secondary drying, which represents the subsequent phase of the freeze-drying process. During secondary drying the temperature is increased, maintaining the high vacuum conditions, to promote this desorption process.

The lyophilization process has been studied from both an economic (Stratta et al. 2020) and an environmental sustainability (Gamiz 2019) perspective. These studies concluded that primary drying is the process phase involving the highest resource consumption, and that shortening primary drying would therefore have the greatest impact on economic efficiency and sustainability.

In this context, the use of mathematical models has emerged as a powerful tool to guide the selection of primary drying process conditions and therefore optimize efficiency (Tchessalov et al. 2023). Several mechanistic models have emerged over the years (Karel 1975, Pikal et al. 1984, Pikal 1985, Liapis and Bruttini 1995, Trelea et al. 2007, Pisano et al. 2011a) and the application of such models in process scale-up and transfer has also been discussed (Trappler 2001, Jennings 2002, Tchessalov et al. 2007, Tchessalov et al. 2017).

The main aim of these models is the identification of primary drying conditions, in particular chamber pressure and shelf temperature, that minimize cycle times, while still resulting in acceptable appearance of the final product (Patel et al. 2017). For this purpose, the models aim at calculating three crucial attributes of the primary drying process, i.e., i) the product temperature, ii) the water vapor mass flux during primary drying, and iii) the time required to complete the sublimation process.

These three attributes are related to three corresponding failure modes of the primary drying phase. First, the sublimation interface temperature should remain below a formulation specific critical value (known as collapse temperature for amorphous materials or melting temperature for crystalline ones) in order to preserve the integrity of the porous structure within the dried product (Pikal and Shah 1990).

Second, the water vapor mass flux during sublimation is constrained by the design of the freeze-dryer. The water vapor that is removed from the vials is conveyed to the condenser, where desublimation occurs. The water vapor molecules accelerate in presence of restrictions along the path to the condenser, but their velocity cannot exceed the speed of sound. When this condition is reached, the flow is said to be choked, and any additional increase in water vapor flow rate results in an uncontrolled increase in chamber pressure (Searles 2004, Patel et al. 2010a). A similar loss in chamber pressure control is encountered when the water vapor flow rate exceeds the maximum deposition capacity of the condenser (ice accumulation capacity as defined in (Ganguly and Alexeenko 2012)), resulting in an uncontrolled increase of the condenser temperature. Both phenomena represent limits of the freeze-drying equipment.

Lastly, the primary drying time should be long enough to ensure that sublimation is complete within the whole batch before switching to secondary drying. If sublimation is not complete before the start of the ramp to the secondary drying phase, the so-called meltback occurs. During meltback, residual ice will melt and dissolve parts of the established dried product. This translates into poor product appearance and higher moisture levels (FDA 2014).

Besides helping to avoid these undesired failure modes of primary drying, modelling can also support regulatory submissions. The lack of a scientific rationale behind the selection of lyophilization cycle conditions is one of the most commonly cited manufacturing-related deficiencies identified in new drug applications for lyophilized injectable products (FDA 2014, Rhieu et al. 2020). Mathematical models can provide the necessary understanding to substantiate the selection of the process conditions.

At last, models are an integral part of the Quality-by-Design (QbD) framework (Patel and Pikal 2013, Yu et al. 2014). They support in process understanding by quantifying the relationship between critical process parameters (CPPs), critical material attributes (CMAs), and critical quality attributes (CQAs) of the drug product. For instance, models can support building a design space (DSp). The DSp is a graphical representation of the CPPs settings that result in acceptable CQAs, with the inclusion of boundaries defined by formulation properties and equipment limitations. It is especially useful when interaction effects exist between different CPPs, and the acceptable range of one CPP depends on the setting of another CPP or CMA.

Several DSp’s for lyophilization have been described in the literature (Chang and Fischer 1995, Nail and Searles 2008, Fissore et al. 2011, Scutella and Bourles 2020, Srinivasan et al. 2023), but these DSp’s have not always been demonstrated to provide assurance of quality. Assurance of quality is instead needed to define a DSp in the regulatory sense (ICH 2009). When a DSp includes risk assessment and is experimentally verified it can provide that quality assurance. Moreover, a change in normal operating ranges (NORs) within the DSp is not considered as a change to the marketing authorization dossier and can be managed without regulatory action (ICH 2009, EMA 2013). In the context of this manuscript we consider that the presented DSp is regulatory compliant. A closely related idea to the DSp concept is the notion of proven acceptable ranges (PARs). A PAR is defined as a characterized range of a CPP for which operation within this range, while keeping other CPPs constant, will result in acceptable CQAs (ICH 2009). Also in this case, the advantage of identifying PARs is that a change in process conditions within the approved PARs can be managed without regulatory action (EMA 2017). Whenever no DSp or PARs are available, only the target operating conditions, and the corresponding NOR presented in the marketing authorization dossier are allowed. The NOR describes the region around the target operating conditions that contain common operational variability. The NOR, in contrast to PAR, is not intended to introduce flexibility in the conditions for manufacturing (EMA 2017).

Mechanistic primary drying models have already proven to be extremely useful in identifying optimal CPPs, supporting scale up and process transfer as well as building the DSp. However, a weakness of current models is the ability to robustly deal with process variability. Primary drying is intrinsically affected by variability (Pikal et al. 2018). As an example, the ice crystals formed during freezing turn into the pores of the dried layer during primary drying. The size and interconnectivity of such pores determines the resistance offered by the dried layer to the removal of water vapor from the sublimation front. Such resistance, generally indicated as Rp in most primary drying models, therefore shows variability within the batch, as freezing is a stochastic process, and the ice crystal size and shape varies between different vials (Arsiccio et al. 2017, Nuytten et al. 2021).

Similarly, the heat transfer towards different vials, quantified via the heat transfer coefficient Kv, is also neither uniformly nor normally distributed within the batch, due to the different contribution of radiation in edge versus central vials (Ehlers et al. 2021, Fontana et al. 2023). The size of the vials and the filling volume also show a distribution due to variabilities of the manufacturing and filling process. Finally, the CPPs such as shelf temperature and chamber pressure are characterized by temporal and spatial variability within their NOR, which are dictated by the controller and the design of the freeze-dryer.

The authors already discussed how to take this variability into consideration in a primary drying model by applying a Monte Carlo uncertainty analysis (Vanbillemont et al. 2023). In this work we further extend this statistical approach and show how it can be incorporated into the calculation of the DSp. We suggest a new procedure to accurately estimate the variability of the resistance to mass transfer (Rp) while iteratively building and verifying the DSp. Our design space approach for primary drying, contrary to previously published ones, includes an uncertainty analysis to consider process variability, therefore showing the probability of failure (PoF) for each combination of the CPPs. This allows a robust evaluation of PARs or the DSp, in line with regulatory requirements. We prove how this DSp, including uncertainty analysis, can be used to guide both cycle optimization and process transfer. In addition, we show how to precisely perform verification and validation (V&V) activities for the developed primary drying model to enhance model credibility, in accordance with the ASME V&V 40–2018 guideline (ASME 2018).

Materials and methods

Mechanistic model structure

A mechanistic model of the primary drying step of a pharmaceutical lyophilization process on the vial level was based on a heat-mass balance coded in Python v3.10.6. The governing equations (Eqs. 1 till 7) were inspired by the work of Pikal (Pikal et al. 1984). Equation 1 is typically called a Kv-Rp model in literature (Leys et al. 2020, Tchessalov et al. 2021, Vanbillemont et al. 2020a) since it employs a heat transfer coefficient (Kv) to describe the heat interaction between the vial and freeze-dryer and a resistance to mass transfer (Rp) to explain the flow restriction of the newly formed dried layer towards the escaping vapor flow.

$${P}_{i}=-\frac{-{{A}_{p}\Delta H}_{sub} {P}_{c}-{A}_{v }{K}_{v }{R}_{p} M({T}_{s}-{T}_{i}-\Delta T)}{{A}_{p} {\Delta H}_{sub}}$$
(1)
$${P}_{i}={\text{A}}_{{P}_{i}}-\frac{{\text{B}}_{{P}_{i}}}{{T}_{i}}+{C}_{{P}_{i}} \text{ln}\left({T}_{i}\right)-{D}_{{P}_{i}}{ T}_{i}$$
(2)
$$\Delta T=\frac{a\frac{{(L}_{t}-{L}_{d})({P}_{i}-{P}_{c})}{{R}_{p}}-b\left({L}_{t}-{L}_{d}\right)\left({T}_{i}-{T}_{s}\right)}{1-b\left({L}_{t}-{L}_{d}\right)}$$
(3)
$${\Delta H}_{sub}={A}_{{H}_{sub}}+{B}_{{H}_{sub}}{ T}_{i}-{C}_{{H}_{sub}} {{T}_{i}}^{2}+{D}_{{H}_{sub}} {e}^{{-\left(\frac{{T}_{i}}{{E}_{{H}_{sub}}}\right)}^{2}}$$
(4)

This set of equations (Eqs. 1 – 4) was simultaneously solved to get an estimate of the sublimation interface temperature (Ti) and temperature difference across the frozen layer (ΔT) in function of CPP’s and CMA’s. CPP’s included the shelf temperature (Ts) and chamber pressure (Pc) since they dictate the primary drying protocol. Product area (Ap), outer vial area (Av), frozen fill height (Lt) and dried layer height (Ld) were considered as CMA’s since they depend on the vial dimensions. Pi represents the partial vapor pressure over ice and its relationship with temperature was described by the APi till DPi coefficients (Mortier et al. 2016). The dependence of the latent heat of sublimation (ΔHsub) with temperature was expressed using the AHsub till EHsub coefficients (Murphy and Koop 2005) and M represents the molecular weight of water. At last, a and b are coefficients that describe the heat conduction across the frozen product layer (Tang et al. 2006).

The progress of sublimation was estimated through Eqs. 5 till 6 by computing the sublimation rate (msub), applying the ice density (ρice) and estimated dried product layer porosity (φ). Completion of primary drying (i.e., Dried samples) was assumed when Ld reached Lt. The vial inner (di) and outer diameters (do) were used to estimate Ap and Av, respectively. Moreover, the fill volume (Vfill) was used to approximate Lt by accounting for the volume expansion by ice formation according to Eq. 7, where ρwater is the density of water.

$${m}_{sub}= \frac{{A}_{p}({P}_{i}- {P}_{c})}{{R}_{p}}$$
(5)
$${L}_{d}= \frac{\int {m}_{sub}dt}{{{\rho }_{ice} \varphi A}_{p}}$$
(6)
$${L}_{t}= \left({V}_{fill}\left(1-\varphi \right)+{V}_{fill} \varphi \frac{ {\rho }_{water} }{{\rho }_{ice}}\right)/{A}_{p}$$
(7)

Risk assessment using statistical sampling

An uncertainty analysis methodology was employed to estimate the risks of the three main failure modes (i.e., collapse, meltback and exceeding the equipment limits) during primary drying. The primary drying phase of a predefined number of samples (nsamples) was simulated by assigning each of these samples with individual CPPs, CMAs, Kv, Rp and Ld values from their expected distributions according to Table 1. These input values were propagated through the mechanistic model. At each integration step a distribution of Ti, distribution of the number of Dried samples (ndried) and ∑msub was achieved. The total sublimation rate of the batch (∑msub) was calculated by multiplying the vial load (nvials) with the averaged simulated sublimation rate at sample level (m̄sub). When a sample reached Dried sample status, it was excluded from the uncertainty analysis and only the remaining samples were advanced to the next integration step.

A risk assessment was conducted using a statistical sampling method on the outputs of the uncertainty analysis, estimating the risks of the three failure modes. The failure mode of collapse was defined as Ti being equal or surpassing the critical temperature (Tc). At each integration step the percentage of samples with collapse was determined (i.e., p(collapse)). Moreover, when a sample reached collapse status it retained it for the remainder of the analysis. Next, the failure mode of meltback was defined as samples still containing a frozen product layer at end of primary drying and could be estimated by the percentage of samples still inside the uncertainty analysis (i.e., p(meltback)). At last, the failure mode of equipment limits (i.e., p(equipment)) was determined by comparing the ∑msub with sublimation rates sustained under minimal controllable chamber pressures (msub_max) inside the variability of the NOR.

Primary drying dynamics were simulated using the uncertainty analysis methodology on an operational process space. The operational process space was described by a predetermined array with size 10 × 10 of primary drying protocols defined by target Ts and Pc. For each of these 100 primary drying protocols the risks of the three failure modes (i.e., p(collapse), p(meltback) and p(equipment)) were determined. A log-linear surface response model was fitted to each of the three risks matrices in function of the target Ts and Pc. These risks were subsequently interpolated to higher resolution operational process space matrices of primary drying protocols with a Ts resolution of 1 °C and Pc resolution of 1 Pa. The total risk of failure p(failure) for the protocols was subsequently computed by taking the union of the three interpolated risks. At last, the process robustness space was constructed by intersecting the p(failure) matrix with the accepted probability of failure (PoF). A PoF of 0.1% was assumed in this work, meaning 1 vial in 1,000 was accepted to display a failure mode. The area within the process robustness space where p(failure) < PoF was colored in shades of blue and represented the primary drying protocols that were defined as robust and thus constitutes a DSp.

The optimal protocol at NOR was selected by repeated binary erosion of the DSp matrix till an isolated protocol was obtained. When multiple isolated protocols were obtained, the protocol with lowest p(failure) was chosen. This approach guaranteed the selection of the NOR with largest associated PARs. The PARs were defined as the univariate ranges of Ts and Pc that extend from the NOR till the edges of the DSp.

Dynamic visualizations

A dynamic dashboard was created in TIBCO Spotfire Analyst 12.0.3 (California, USA) to import and process the raw process data from the freeze-dryer. Data processing included the necessary transformations and slicing of the data into the different freeze-drying phases such as freezing, primary drying and secondary drying. The mechanistic primary drying model, risk assessment and Rp extraction were programmed in Python as data functions within the dashboard to allow easy interaction with the dataset. The primary drying process data was used to estimate the Rp parameters. The dashboard allowed Rp regression from multiple lyophilization cycles of the same product to get a more accurate estimation of the Rp profile and its variability.

Model credibility

To enhance the trust in the predictive capabilities of the mechanistic model and risk assessment methodology as described above, several model verification and validation (V&V) activities were performed. These V&V activities were based on the ASME V&V 40–2018 guidelines (ASME 2018). Despite the fact that the application field of this guideline is medical devices, it is the opinion of the authors that the methodology described in this guideline can be applied to pharmaceutical products as well, to enhance model credibility.

The proposed risk assessment methodology can be applied to support several drug product development activities for lyophilized products. Experimental validation of the first three use cases as outlined below are discussed within this work.

Envisioned use cases of the methodology

  • Optimization of the primary drying process with the main aim to reduce process time

  • Assessment of process robustness

  • Support in scale-up or process transfer between lyophilizers

  • Support in root-cause analysis of primary drying process failures

Code and calculation verification

Code verification and calculation verification activities were performed on the final version of the dynamic dashboard including all data functions. Code verification involved inventorying and assessing the quality of all software packages, interpreters and Python libraries that were applied. Next, a second operator check was performed on all in-house built code to remove errors in the source code and increase readability.

In a next step, calculation verification was performed on the risk assessment methodology. The impact of two numerical parameters were evaluated on the accuracy of the results: the integration timestep (dt) and number of samples (nsamples). For this purpose, the same case study as described in section 3.3.5 was used for a single primary drying protocol at Pc 8 Pa and Ts −24 °C. The default dt was defined as 1/500th of the primary drying time predicted using nominal parameter settings. This ensures that the primary drying dynamics are covered by at least 500 equidistant datapoints. To achieve a statistically relevant number of samples the default nsamples was fixed at 4,096. The analytical truth was calculated using 1/25 th of the default dt and 16-fold the default nsamples. Next, to investigate the model sensitivity towards dt, a series of predictions were performed between 1/25 th and 25-fold of the default dt while keeping nsamples at the default value. Similarly, predictions were made using 16-fold till 1/16 th of the default nsamples while keeping the default dt. The error in comparison to the analytical truth was calculated for the three main outcomes of the model (i.e., maximum Ti, primary drying time and equipment limits) to evaluate the ideal setting of nsamples and dt.

Model validation

Materials

Model validation experiments were conducted using a biopharmaceutical model formulation comprising of 10 mM Tris (Emprove Expert, Merck) at pH 7.3, 225 mM sucrose (Emprove Expert, Merck), 25 mg/ml BSA (≥ 98%, Carl Roth) and 0.01% polysorbate 20 (PS20) (Super Refined, Croda Pharma). The formulation has an estimated porosity (φ) of 89.7% in the dried state, and a protein-free theoretical osmolality of 265 mOsmol/kg.

6R type I glass vials (Fiolax standard line, Schott), 20 mm bromobutyl stoppers with Flurotec coating stoppers (B2-TR Westar, West) and 20 mm crimp caps (Adelphi Healthcare Packaging) were used as primary packaging material.

Two freeze-dryers from Martin Christ (Osterode, Germany) were used in the model validation experiments. Cycle optimization and a process robustness assessment were performed at the scale-1 pilot freeze-dryer, while process transfer to a larger equipment was performed on the scale-2 commercial freeze-dryer as summarized in Table 2.

Model input parameter variability estimation

Variation from the target CPPs can be expected due to the use of process controllers in the freeze-dryer. Moreover, a distribution of pressure across the chamber and a temperature distribution across the shelves are reported in literature (Strongrich and Alexeenko 2021, Rasetto et al. 2010). To account for these variations, a uniform distribution with a limit of ± 1 °C for Ts (Ts_lim) and ± 1 Pa (Pc_lim) for Pc was chosen. The NORs were therefore defined as the target primary drying protocol enlarged by the Ts_lim and Pc_lim.

A complete shelf at scale-1 of 6R vials (i.e., 257 vials) was characterized for their inner (di) and outer diameters (do) to determine the averages (d̄I and d̄o) and standard deviations (σdi and σdo), assuming normal distributions (see Fig. 1). Next, these vials were filled with 2.5 ml (approximately 8 mm liquid filling height) of water for the Kv determination. The filling volume accuracy by means of the standard deviation (σVfill) was determined gravimetrically on these vials.

The Kv distribution of 6R vials for the scale-1 and scale-2 freeze-dryer was determined according to the methodology described in Vanbillemont et al. (Vanbillemont et al. 2023). The vials were assigned to different groups according to their position on the shelf, and at least one product temperature sensor per vial group was used as indicated in Fig. 1. The sensors were placed at the bottom center of the vial, touching the glass. Short freeze-drying cycles on at least three different pressure levels between 5 and 25 Pa, were executed with the vials filled with water. These sublimation cycles were interrupted after 3 h to determine the loss-in-weight (\(\int {m}_{sub}dt\)) of each individual vial allowing a vial-specific Kv estimation using the group-specific bottom product temperature profile (Tb) by applying Eq. 8.

$${K}_{v}= \frac{{\int {m}_{sub}dt\Delta H}_{sub}}{{A}_{v} M\int ({T}_{s}-{T}_{b})dt}$$
(8)

The shelf average heat transfer coefficient (K̅v) was regressed against Pc by estimating coefficients AKv, BKv and CKv using a weighted non-linear regression according to Eq. 9, with the inverse of the standard deviation at each pressure level as the weights (Vanbillemont et al. 2020b). As a measure for variability of the Kv dataset, the relative standard deviation (RSDKv) was calculated by pooling the RSD at each pressure level.

$${\overline{K} }_{v}={A}_{Kv}+ \frac{{{B}_{Kv} P}_{c}}{1+{C}_{Kv}{ P}_{c}}$$
(9)

An empirical cumulative distribution (ECD) of normalized heat transfer coefficients (K̂v) was created by applying Eq. 10 in order to pool all Kv datapoints across the different pressure levels.

$${\widehat K}_v=\frac{K_v\left(P_c\right)-{\overline K}_v\left(P_c\right)}{\sigma_{K_v}\left(P_c\right)}$$
(10)

To reconstruct a Kv distribution as an input for the uncertainty analysis, the ECD with K̂v values was reverted to Kv values by using the K̅v and RSDKv.

Constraints estimation

Each of the three failure modes was defined by constraining parameters which limited the operational process space to achieve the risk assessment. The critical temperature of the formulation (Tc) influenced the p(collapse). The Tc was determined using a FDSC 196 freeze-drying microscopy (FDM) (Linkam, Surray, UK) with 10 × magnification applying a cooling ramp of 10 °C/min to −50 °C, followed by a heating ramp of 1 °C/min at 10 Pa. Next, the p(meltback) was determined by setting a predefined target primary drying time (PD time).

At last, to determine the p(equipment) the maximal sublimation rate (msub_max) was measured under either choked flow or condenser deposition capacity conditions. Minimal controllable pressure experiments inspired by Srinivasan et al. (Srinivasan et al. 2021) were executed. Bottomless trays were lined with thin plastic sheets and filled with water to form ice slabs upon freezing. Each tray contained approximately 1.2 L (about 1 cm fill height) of deionized water. Each tray was equipped with a product temperature sensor and three short lyophilization cycles at a controlled Pc of 5, 28 and 50 Pa were executed where the loss-in-weight of the ice slabs were determined. The Kv of this system was computed using Eq. 8 and regressed in function of Pc according to Eq. 9. Next, several lyophilization runs were executed with the ice slabs, where the target Pc was set to 0.1 Pa, which was well below the minimal controllable pressure of the lyophilizer. During these runs the Ts was varied between−60 °C and 40 °C in steps of 10 °C, with a holding time of 2 h at each Ts. The resulting Pc at the end of the 2 h holding steps was considered as the minimal controllable pressure and the maximal sublimation rate (msub_max) at that point was estimated using the Kv of the ice slab system. The Pc dependent msub_max datapoints were considered to be limited by a choked flow regime. A linear regression was made though these msub_max datapoints to estimate the intercept (aCF) and slope (bCF) in function of Pc. The Pc independent msub_max datapoints were attributed to a condenser deposition capacity limit and were used to obtain msub_Cond.

DSp building and verification

The dried product layer resistance (Rp) versus dried layer length (Ld) profile is dependent on the formulation and freezing protocol, as it describes the interaction of the escaping water vapor with the microstructure of the developing dried product layer. The Rp profiles applied in the model were extracted from recorded process data by fitting Eqs. 1 till 8. Vial-group-specific Kv coefficients considering the position of the temperature monitored vials were applied. Rp values that were negative or values near the end of primary drying that showed an exponential relationship to Ld were omitted since they were not representative of the assumption of a fully planar sublimation front. Rp versus Ld profiles originating from multiple temperature monitored vials were aggregated. Next, an averaged dried product layer resistance (R̅p) profile was regressed according to Eq. 11, by determining the Rp coefficients (i.e., ARp, BRp and CRp) (Scutella et al. 2018). At last, the relative standard deviation (RSDRp) was determined by pooling the RSDs from the Rp values of 200 equidistant Ld bins of the different temperature monitored vials (Vanbillemont et al. 2020c).

$${\overline{R} }_{p}={A}_{Rp}+ \frac{{{B}_{Rp} L}_{d}}{1+{C}_{Rp} {L}_{d}}$$
(11)

A conservative freeze-drying process was first executed to have an initial estimate of the Rp parameters describing the Rp profile, and its variability across the batch. The freezing step of this conservative process comprised a cooling ramp of 1 °C/min till −45 °C, followed by a holding step of 2 h at −45 °C. Next, primary drying started with a vacuum ramp till 10 Pa within 30 min at −45 °C. Subsequently, a ramp of 1 °C/min till −28 °C was programmed and held for 67 h. At last, secondary drying included a slow ramp of 0.12 °C/min till 30 °C, followed by a holding step at 30 °C for 8 h at the same chamber pressure setting as primary drying.

A risk assessment was performed applying the preliminary Rp parameters from the conservative cycle. Hence forward an iterative approach was conducted to determine and verify the edges of robustness. The outcomes of the last risk assessment were used to identify the primary drying protocols associated with an edge of robustness, i.e., at the intersection point between two different failure modes. Next, that specific primary drying protocol was validated experimentally, and the Rp parameters were updated by regression on the aggregation of all Rp profiles from the previous processes. At last, a new risk assessment was constructed using the updated Rp parameters to estimate the next edge of robustness. The first edge protocol that was explored was at the intersection of collapse and equipment limits (i.e., p(collapse) U p(equipment) < PoF). Next, the intersection of collapse and meltback was investigated. At last, the edge of robustness at the intersection of meltback and equipment limits was verified. In essence, these three runs at the edges of robustness were a verification of the DSp. At the end, the most robust point within the DSp, was considered as the optimal protocol at NOR for the scale-1 freeze-dryer. The optimal primary drying protocol at NOR was experimentally validated and the final Rp parameters were established based on these five freeze-drying cycles.

Process transfer

The main aim of the process transfer was to keep the primary drying kinetics, i.e., the thermal history, as similar as possible between both scales (Tchessalov et al. 2017). The rationale was to adapt CPPs to anticipate the scale dependent CMAs changes in order to maintain the CQAs. The scale dependent CMAs changes in a primary drying process are concentrated in the heat transfer coefficient (Kv) and equipment limits constraints. Therefore, the AKv, BKv, CKv, RSDKv, nvials, aCF, bCF and msub_Cond of the scale-2 freeze-dryer were applied in the calculations. Next, the risk assessment was repeated at scale-2, applying the final Rp parameters from scale-1. The optimal protocol at NOR for the freeze-dryer scale-2 was chosen as the point within the risk assessment matrix (i.e., the unverified DSp) with comparable primary drying kinetics as scale-1. This optimal primary drying protocol was finally validated experimentally on the scale-2 freeze-dryer. Since the lower Pc process space in the risk assessment was not limited by choked flow limits but rather due to the model calibration limits (i.e., 5 till 25 Pa), only one full shelf of the scale-2 freeze-dryer was loaded in this validation experiment.

Morphological quality attributes

The morphological appearance of the lyophilized cakes was evaluated according to an image-based method, designed to identify cake shrinkage, meltback or collapse (Patel et al. 2017) and minimize operator bias. For this purpose, eight high-resolution pictures of the lyophilized vials were taken from the front side at each 45° rotation angle. The images were converted to grayscale and subsequently binarized using Otzu’s thresholding method (Otsu 1979). After binarization, the lyophilized cake was represented by white pixels, while the vial wall and background by black pixels. The fraction of white pixels in each image was then counted, therefore making it possible to define a cake volume fraction, by averaging over the eight collected pictures. Twenty-six vials were selected from different batch positions after each lyophilization run, and morphologically characterized using this approach, to obtain a distribution of cake volume fraction across the whole batch. Exemplary pictures of this methodology are provided in the supplementary information (Figure S1).

Residual water content

The residual water content of the freeze-dried products was determined by using the coulometric Karl Fischer titrator Aqua 40.00 (Analytik Jena GmbH), which is equipped with a headspace module. The sample preparation of the freeze-dried product was performed in a glovebox under controlled humidity (≤ 10% r.h.).

For the measurements, about 20–30 mg of freeze-dried product were weighed into 2R glass vials (Schott, Mainz, Germany) and heated to 120 °C in the oven connected to the reaction vessel via a tubing system. The evaporated water was transferred into the titration solution and the amount of water was determined. The measurement was performed until no more water evaporation was detectable (the baseline was determined by the drift before a measurement series, and the stop criteria by the oscillation around the baseline). The water content of the sample was calculated by taking the environmental moisture as determined in three blanks (i.e., empty vials prepared in the measurement environment and analyzed by using the same method and settings as the sample) into consideration.

Results and discussion

Model context

According to the ASME V&V 40–2018 guidelines (ASME 2018), the first step which is needed to assess the credibility of a model is a clear identification of the questions of interest (QOI) that the model is trying to answer, and of the corresponding context of use (COU). The following QOIs were identified for the mechanistic model of primary drying discussed in the present work:

  1. 1.

    Finding optimal CPPs (i.e., combination of Ts, Pc and PD time) therefore defining the NOR for the primary drying phase of lyophilization.

  2. 2.

    Proving robustness of the optimal CPPs by establishing a DSp.

  3. 3.

    Assisting in transfer of a lyophilization primary drying protocol between different equipment.

In reference to the above-mentioned QOIs 1–3, the following COUs apply:

  1. 1.

    Mass and heat balance equations are applied to the primary drying phase of lyophilization, and an uncertainty analysis based on distribution of input variables and model parameters is also performed. A matrix of chamber pressure and shelf temperature values is simulated with different process time limits and the probability of different failure modes (collapse, meltback and exceeding the equipment limits) is computed for each element of the matrix. This allows the selection of optimal CPPs (QOI #1). The model can further be used to transfer a lyophilization primary drying protocol to a different scale (scale-2) by measuring scale-dependent model parameters (heat transfer coefficient and equipment limits) at scale-2, and by re-applying the model (QOI #3). A robustness assessment (QOI #2) is performed and the area of robustness surrounding the optimal CPPs is validated experimentally. This results in the generation of a DSp, i.e., a representation allowing the identification of a robustness area around the selected CPPs. In this case, any change in CPPs within the verified DSp can be managed without regulatory action.

  2. 2.

    The same procedure for the identification of the optimal CPPs at scale 1 and/or 2 is applied, as discussed in COU #1. However, process robustness (QOI #2) is not fully assessed at commercial scale, and only the optimal cycle is validated experimentally. In this case, the model generates an unverified DSp, and the only accepted variability for the optimal CPPs is around their NORs. This is sufficient for submission of an application (EMA 2013), but an additional post-approval DSp verification effort should be performed if the operational flexibility of a DSp approach is desired.

The next step for V&V activities, according to the ASME V&V 40–2018 guidelines, is the model risk classification. When the COU is clear, the risk associated with the application of the model can be assessed based on the model influence and decision consequence, according to the classification scheme shown in Table 3.

For both COUs discussed in this work, the model influence is classified as medium, as the model output is used to guide decisions on CPPs, but experimental verification is used to confirm the model output and drive the final decision. Furthermore, the decision consequence is ranked as low, because appropriate experimental responses (i.e., the CQAs) are selected in the verification experiment to capture potential adverse effects in quality, safety or efficacy of the drug product (DP). Moreover, the appropriate experimental responses are determined across the whole regulatory design space at scale-1 (COU #1) and at the NOR at scale-2 (COU #2).

The model risk is therefore rated as low (in alignment with (ASME 2018) and (EMA 2013)), as illustrated in Fig. 2. Since model risk is rated as low, a limited set of V&V activities is appropriate, as discussed in the following sections.

Code and calculation verification

A code and calculation verification on the final version of the dynamic dashboard, including all data functions, was subsequently performed. The model used the built-in IronPython (v. 2.7) and Python (v. 3.10.6) interpreters in TIBCO Spotfire Analyst 12.0.3. The Python packages used included distfit 1.7.3, joblib 1.3.2, numpy 1.24.1, olefile 0.47, pandas 1.5.2, scikit-learn 1.4.0, scipy 1.12.0, statsmodels 0.14.1 and xlrd 2.0.1. TIBCO Spotfire and Python are established software, and no additional software quality assurance was performed as part of the current code and calculation verification.

As discussed in the Materials and Methods section, an analysis of the effect of the timestep dt and the number of samples nsamples on the accuracy of the model predictions was performed. The result of this investigation is shown in Fig. 3. From Fig. 3 it is evident that nsamples has a much lower impact on the accuracy than dt (left vs. right panel of Fig. 3). Changing nsamples in the range 16x (i.e., 65,536) till 1/8x (i.e., 512) resulted in small error values (< 1%) on all model outputs, and only at the most extreme condition tested (1/16x, nsamples: 256) the error increases above 1% as illustrated in Fig. 3.

To the contrary, increasing dt resulted in a significant decrease in accuracy, with errors up to 18% on the predicted equipment limits when dt is increased to 5 × the default (i.e., 1,320 s).

The analysis performed here also suggests that the default dt and nsamples result in acceptably small errors (< 1%) compared to the reference (1/25 × dt, 16 × nsamples) simulation. It should also be considered that the modelling approach used for the process robustness assessment involves random sampling of the nsamples input vectors, meaning that some variability of the output data is to be expected. The output variability due to the random sampling procedure was quantified by repeating the same calculation 6 times using the default timestep dt and number of samples nsamples. As a result of this analysis, relative standard deviations of 0.08%, 0.42% and 0.19% were obtained on the predicted maximum temperature, primary drying time and equipment capability limits, respectively. Based on this analysis, the variability on model outputs due to statistical sampling is of the same order of magnitude as the error between the default (1 × dt, 1 × nsamples) and the reference (1/25 × dt, 16 × nsamples) simulation, further suggesting that the default solver parameters are adequate to ensure accuracy. Interestingly, the highest relative standard deviation was observed on the predicted primary drying time. This is not surprising since primary drying time predictions suffer from the error propagation in the estimation of the dried layer length. Meltback predictions are based on primary drying time estimations, and therefore the highest uncertainty on the design space calculation is expected to occur on the edge of failure due to meltback.

Model validation

CMAs variability

Container related CMAs were determined experimentally. First, the dimensions of the 6R vials in terms of di and do were normally distributed with average values approximating the ISO 8321–1 specifications of 20.0 and 22.0 mm. The standard deviations σdi and σdo were 0.13 and 0.10 mm, respectively. Next, the target fill volume of 2.50 ml was applied in the model and a filling variability (σVfill) of 0.09 ml was obtained from the gravimetric experiments.

As extensively described in the literature (Pisano et al. 2011b) and illustrated in Fig. 4A, the shelf average heat transfer coefficient (K̅v) showed a dependency on the chamber pressure Pc. This dependency can be explained by gas convection (Pisano et al. 2011b) and was adequately described for the scale-1 freeze-dryer using an AKv of 2.57 W/(m2 K), BKv of 2.33 W/(m2 K Pa), CKv of 0.03 1/Pa, RSDKv of 15.1% and for the scale-2 freeze-dryer with an AKv of 8.83 W/(m2 K) or, BKv of 0.60 W/(m2 K Pa), CKv 0.00 1/Pa and RSDKv of 11.0%. At a given value of chamber pressure Pc, the heat transfer coefficient Kv of the 6R vials in the scale-1 freeze-dryer was not uniform within the batch, but showed a bimodal distribution with strong tailing to higher values as depicted in Fig. 4B. This extensive inhomogeneity originates mainly from differences in radiation and, to a minor extent, conduction. The center vials in groups 5 and 6 (see Fig. 1) were the main population in the distribution, located at lower K̂v values. These vials display six direct neighbors that are similarly cooled by sublimation and are therefore partially shielded from radiation of uncooled surfaces, explaining the lower Kv (Ehlers et al. 2021). Vials in groups 4, 3 and 2 have successively less of these neighboring vials, as they are situated near or at the edge of the vial pack, resulting in a higher Kv. At last, vials in group 1 are the corner vials and are therefore very atypical (i.e., K̂v between 2.5 till 4.6 σ), as they have limited contact with the rest of the pack. An empirical cumulative distribution similar to the one shown in Fig. 4B was also constructed for the scale-2 freeze-dryer and 6R vial combination.

Constraints estimations

The constraints due to the drug product (i.e., the critical temperature of the formulation) and the equipment (i.e., the choked flow and condenser deposition capacity limits) play a role in determining the robustness of a primary drying protocol. Freeze-drying microscopy (FDM) identified the collapse temperature of the model formulation at −28 °C, which was therefore defined as the Tc. A representative image collected during the FDM experiment is displayed in Fig. 5A.

Minimal controllable pressure experiments were performed to identify the choked flow and condenser capacity limits of both freeze-dryers (Fig. 5B). Clear differences between the freeze-dryers can be observed. The pilot scale freeze-dryer (scale-1) displayed in red in Fig. 5B appears to be overdesigned in relation to the shelf-area-normalized equipment limits when comparing it to the commercial scale (scale-2) freeze-dryer shown in blue. This observation was already reported before (Shivkumar 2022). It poses serious risks when a process optimization is conducted at lab or pilot scale, and later on transferred to a commercial scale. This occurs because the pilot scale freeze-dryer can sustain higher sublimation rates in relation to its vial capacity, possibly leading to a design of aggressive primary drying protocol not attainable on the commercial scale.

Two distinct regimes could be identified from the minimal controllable pressure experiments. A Pc dependent regime at lower msub_max values, which stabilized into a Pc independent regime, as can be observed in Fig. 5B. The Pc dependence was observed up to 19 Pa and 2.5 Pa for the scale-1 and scale-2 freeze-dryers, respectively. This regime was attributed to choked flow at the restriction between the drying chamber and condenser chamber. The scale-1 freeze-dryer had a clear duct between the two chambers, whereas the scale-2 freeze-dryer had the condenser coils below the stoppering plate. The choked-flow regime of the scale-2 freeze-dryer was located at the Pc range till 2.5 Pa, below the Pc calibration range of the model, due to limited restriction of the vapor flow. The choked flow regime could be described by an aCF of 0.10 or 0.03 kg/h and a bCF of 0.33 or 0.60 kg/(h Pa) for the scale-1 and scale-2 freeze-dryers, respectively.

The Pc independent regime was associated with escalating condenser temperatures. When the msub_max reached the condenser capacity (msub_Cond), the heat of ice deposition could not completely be removed and the resulting rising condenser temperature affected the Pc according to the vapor pressure over ice equilibrium. For scale-2, the msub_Cond was estimated at 1.49 kg/h. The condenser capacity regime could not be reached experimentally for scale-1 since the highest Ts setting of 40 °C was still located in the choked flow regime. The condenser capacity for scale-1 was specified by the manufacturer at msub_Cond = 0.33 kg/h.

Conservative lyophilization cycle for preliminary R p determination

After the determination of CMAs variability, product and equipment constraints, the remaining input for the model is the resistance to mass transfer Rp. For this purpose, a conservative lyophilization run was conducted (process data is shown in Fig. 6A). A Ts equal to Tc at −28 °C was chosen for this cycle to eliminate the risk of collapse. Temperature probes were placed inside center vials, and preliminary regression coefficients for Rp were obtained from product temperature measurements (Fig. 6B). Figure 6B shows that the calculated Rp profiles are characterized by substantial variability, due to the stochasticity of freezing, and that three relative standard deviations (RSD) on the regression fit are needed to capture such variability.

Using the model inputs collected so far, a preliminary risk assessment calculation was performed, which allowed the identification of boundaries of process robustness towards collapse, meltback and equipment limits.

Lyophilization cycles for DSp verification

As outlined in the Materials and Methods section, an iterative approach was conducted to determine and verify the edges of robustness. At first, the edge of robustness located at the intersection of collapse and equipment limits was verified experimentally. This choice was made, because this is the only edge that is not dependent on the predetermined PD time. As discussed in the section 3.2, the highest uncertainty on the design space calculation is expected to occur on the edge of failure due to meltback, and for this reason the two edge points delimited by meltback were verified later.

The first edge run was conducted at Ts: −22 °C, Pc: 6 Pa and PD time 45 h (Fig. 7A). The product temperature readouts show a typical sigmoidal shape. At first, the temperature increases to reach a first plateau, where the endothermic process of sublimation is compensated by the heat transfer from the shelf. After this first plateau, a sharp change in slope is observed. This occurs when the sublimation front, in its movement from the top to the bottom of the product, reaches the sensing element of the temperature probe. The probe is not anymore located within the frozen area and measures the increase in temperature within the dried layer instead. When equilibrium is achieved between the different heat transfer modes towards the dried product, a second plateau is reached, which is typically close to or above the shelf temperature, due to radiation effects. All the product temperature readouts were below the Tc of −28 °C during the first plateau, suggesting that the frozen layer never exceeded the critical temperature. The end of sublimation, as marked by the temperature sensor reaching the second plateau, showed a wide distribution within the batch. Corner vials (Edge P1) were the first to dry because of their exposure to radiation, while center vials (e.g., Edge P5 and Edge P6) completed sublimation last.

Product temperature measurements from this first edge run were used to increase the reliability of the Rp coefficients, and to compute new boundaries of robustness towards the different failure modes. This increased confidence in the prediction of the edge of failure due to meltback, and after this first edge run the PD time was prolonged to 50 h. With this in mind, the second edge of robustness, located at the intersection of collapse and meltback, was therefore verified experimentally (Fig. 7B) at a Ts of −25 °C, a Pc of 12 Pa and PD time of 50 h. Also in this case, the product temperature readouts were below the Tc during the first plateau, confirming that the model can accurately predict the effect of operating conditions on the maximum product temperature. The duration of the sublimation phase was likewise below the selected 50-h limit (i.e., below 53.1 h total process time, as the duration of freezing was 3.1 h). This can be inferred from both the second plateau of the product temperature readings, and comparative pressure measurements. This can be inferred from both the second plateau of the product temperature readings, and comparative pressure measurements. Indeed, the Pirani gauge measures pressure by correlating it to the thermal conductivity of the atmosphere within the drying chamber. The Pirani gauge readings during the sublimation phase are above the actual pressure value within the chamber, because the atmosphere within the drying chamber is mostly composed of water vapor, that displays a higher thermal conductivity than nitrogen. A decrease in the Pirani reading is an indication that sublimation is complete in most of the batch. When the Pirani and capacitance manometer readings are aligned, sublimation is complete in the whole batch (Patel et al. 2010b). As shown in Fig. 7B, the Pirani reading converged to the capacitance measurement before the limit process time of 53.1 h.

New values for Rp coefficients were estimated after this second edge run and fed back to the algorithm for risk assessment and robustness prediction. A third edge condition, at the intersection of the meltback and equipment limits failure boundaries, was selected based the improved Rp coefficients estimate. This edge condition, located at Ts of −25 °C, Pc of 6 Pa and PD time of 50 h was also verified experimentally (Fig. 7C). In this case as well, the sublimation phase was completed before the start of the ramp to secondary drying, as indicated by the convergence of Pirani and capacitance readings. It is important to note that in both the edge 1 and edge 3 runs, which are located at the equipment limits boundary, no loss in pressure control was observed, suggesting that the model could control this failure mode, as well.

The CQAs of the lyophilized product, i.e., cake volume fraction and residual water content, were also measured for vials produced in the three edge runs (Fig. 8, black bars). Pronounced cake volume shrinkage, and high residual water content are an indication of either collapse or meltback (Patel et al. 2017). No marked shrinkage was observed after any of the three edge runs, and the residual water content values were in all cases below 0.65%. This points towards the absence of collapse or meltback of the lyophilized cakes obtained from the experiments and verifies the DSp predicted by the model.

Optimal cycle

After the iterative verification of the DSp, optimal cycle conditions were selected within the DSp and as far as possible from the boundaries of failure. Such conditions corresponded to Ts of −24 °C, Pc of 8 Pa and PD time of 50 h. These conditions were defined as the NORs since they included the expected variability of the CPPs (Ts ± 1 °C and Pc ± 1 Pa) as outlined in section 2.6.2. Process readouts from the experimental verification of this target operating condition (Fig. 9A) confirmed that the product temperature was consistently below the collapse limit, and that the sublimation phase was completed before the start of the ramp to secondary drying, as indicated by comparative pressure measurements. Furthermore, the optimized process resulted in a 26% reduction in primary drying time compared to the conservative run performed for Rp extraction (from 68 to 50 h primary drying time).

The CQAs of the lyophilized cakes obtained from the optimal protocol (Fig. 8, red bars) fell within the distributions measured for the three edge runs. This proves that the results from the optimal lyophilization run at NOR are not distinctly different from the proven DSp. In addition, it confirms that the DSp approach is a useful tool to improve performance (e.g., 26% reduction in PD time) without negatively affecting the critical quality attributes of the final product.

Product temperature readings from the optimal lyophilization run were also used to further refine the extraction of Rp coefficients (Fig. 9B), leading to a good fit of the experimental value (root mean square error RMSE of 8.09∙104), and a robust estimation of RSDRp. Final values of ARp, BRp, CRp and RSDRp were 7.00∙103 m/s, 2.77∙108 1/s, 451 1/m and 21.1%, respectively. A final risk assessment calculation was performed at scale-1, using the knowledge on Rp gained from the DSp verification experiments and the optimal run at NOR. The risk assessment matrix obtained (Fig. 10A, computed at 50 h PD time) displays the verified DSp in blue. Changes in Ts and Pc settings within this area result in a p(failure) below 0.1%. The optimal protocol at NOR is marked in green, and the three edge conditions experimentally tested during DSp verification are highlighted and numbered in black.

The conservative protocol for the preliminary determination of Rp (marked in grey) resulted in a sublimation phase which lasted more than 50 h, and for this reason this lyophilization protocol lies outside the meltback boundary of the DSp computed at 50-h PD time.

When fixing Ts at −24 °C, the PAR of Pc goes from 6 to 10 Pa, according to the calculated risk assessment matrix. Similarly, a PAR of −25 °C to −23 °C applies to Ts when fixing Pc at the target value of 8 Pa. This shows that the model herein discussed, and the resulting risk assessment matrix, allows identification of PARs (marked dashed red in Fig. 10A) around the target operating condition. Moreover, the model can support in the construction of the DSp, and the selection of experimental conditions for DSp verification.

Lyophilization process transfer

The risk assessment as shown in Fig. 10B was calculated by applying the scale-2 appropriate Kv and equipment limit parameters, with the final Rp coefficients. Upon comparing the risk assessments at the two scales, it can be observed that the robust blue area shifted by 2 °C on the Ts-axis, and that this area at scale-2 was also slightly enlarged. This shift originated from a slightly worse heat transfer (lower Kv regression in Fig. 4A) in the scale-2 versus scale-1 freeze-dryer. To attain similar primary drying kinetics of the optimal protocols between both scales, the model predicted to adapt the scale-2 CPPs by Ts + 2 °C to sustain the heat flux. The enlargement of the predicted robustness area was driven by the RSDKv (scale-1: 15.1% vs. scale-2: 11.0%). The larger shelf area at scale-2 versus scale-1 resulted in relatively less atypical and outlying vials of groups 1–4 versus the more homogeneous groups 5–6. Furthermore, the scale-1 freeze-dryer displayed a plexiglass door enhancing radiation effects which were less present at scale-2 due to the presence of a stainless-steel door. Both effects contributed to the decrease in RSDKv at scale-2 and therefore to the enlargement of the predicted robustness area. In other words, a higher homogeneity between the samples is expected in scale-2 versus scale-1.

The optimal protocol at Ts of −22 °C, Pc of 8 Pa and PD time of 50 h with associated NORs of ± 1 °C and ± 1 Pa was experimentally verified with its process data displayed in Fig. 11. During this cycle, the product temperature readouts upon sublimation were consistently well below the Tc, the sublimation phase was completed before 50 h of PD time, as indicated by comparative pressure measurements, and Pc was well under control indicating sufficient process robustness. Furthermore, as illustrated by the blue bars in Fig. 8, the distribution of the measured CQAs were comparable between both scales, further evidencing process robustness of the transferred CPPs.

The information supplied in this manuscript would suffice to substantiate the decision of the NORs at the scale-2 freeze-dryer in an application for marketing authorization. However, the blue area in the risk assessment (i.e., Fig. 10B) is an unverified DSp. During the product lifecycle additional experiments at scale-2, analogous to the edge of robustness runs in section 3.3.4, should be performed to verify the suggested DSp. Alternatively, the PARs in the ranges of Pc: 5 till 11 Pa and Ts: −24 till −21 °C could be experimentally verified. However, since the mechanistic primary drying model suggests numerous interactions between Ts and Pc it is the opinion of the authors that the DSp approach is superior.

Conclusions

In the present work, a modelling framework to increase the robustness of process parameters selection for the primary drying phase of freeze-drying has been developed and discussed. The model performs an uncertainty analysis on the input parameters of a primary drying process and propagates such uncertainty to estimate the distribution of the CQAs. By doing this, the model can estimate the risk associated with different failure modes, such as collapse, meltback or exceeding the equipment limits. The possibility of using the model for transferring process parameters between different equipment, e.g., during process scale-up, has also been discussed.

We have further shown how V&V activities can be performed to increase model credibility. Our model validation experiments, performed on a representative biopharmaceutical formulation, confirmed that the model can robustly predict the combination of CPPs leading to acceptable CQAs. This leads to the generation of a risk assessment matrix, i.e., a visual representation of the impact of CPPs on CQAs. When experimentally verified, the risk assessment matrix becomes a regulatory compliant DSp. The advantage of a DSp approach is that it introduces flexibility in the conditions for manufacturing, as any change in CPPs within the DSp can be managed without regulatory action.

Overall, we think that the primary drying model herein discussed represents an advancement in the execution of uncertainty analysis for process robustness estimation. Furthermore, the framework for model V&V, and for DSp generation that we present streamlines the steps required to increase the credibility of a model, and to adequately include such information in a marketing authorization dossier. Such framework could be extended to different models and different unit operations, as well, as the overall approach and nomenclature we use are generally applicable, and not only limited to the field of freeze-drying.
