Optimization and transfer of robust primary 
drying protocols for biopharmaceuticals 
through lyophilization process modeling
Brecht Vanbillemont1*  , Andrea Arsiccio1, Tim Menzen1 and Andrea Hawe1 
Abstract 
Lyophilization, or freeze-drying, is a widespread process for stabilizing (bio)pharmaceutical products. The primary 
drying phase is resource-intensive, making its optimization a focal point for improving economic and environmental 
sustainability. Mechanistic modeling of the primary drying phase has emerged as a key tool for designing robust 
processes, identifying optimal critical process parameters, and minimizing failure modes such as product collapse, 
uncontrolled pressure increase, or incomplete sublimation. This study extended the traditional modeling frame-
works by incorporating an uncertainty analysis to account for process variability originating from process parameters 
and material attributes. Model validation and verification activities were performed to establish model credibility. The 
ensuing risk assessment quantified the failure mode probabilities. This methodology was capable of ensuring process 
robustness at a pilot scale freeze-dryer by construction of a regulatory compliant design space and the establish-
ment of proven acceptable ranges. An iterative approach to collect process knowledge was conducted by updating 
the dried layer resistance estimates while verifying the design space. The transfer of the normal operating ranges 
to a commercial scale freeze-dryer was assisted by the model and was experimentally verified. Experimental results 
confirmed the model’s capability to predict process parameter combinations yielding acceptable critical quality 
attributes while minimizing process time. The model provides a scientific rationale for process parameters selec-
tion and can support in the process optimization, robustness assessment and process transfer activities necessary 
for a regulatory submission.
Keywords Biopharmaceuticals, Lyophilization, Process transfer, Process robustness, Mechanistic modelling, Process 
optimization, Regulatory submission, Sustainability

Introduction
Lyophilization, or freeze-drying, is commonly used to 
increase the shelf life of (bio)pharmaceuticals. During 
lyophilization, the product is first frozen, and the ice 
crystals are subsequently removed by sublimation during 
the primary drying phase. The removal of ice occurs at 
low temperature and high vacuum conditions during pri -
mary drying, making this process particularly suitable for 
heat-sensitive molecules. The desorption of the unfrozen 
water molecules takes place during secondary drying, 
which represents the subsequent phase of the freeze-dry -
ing process. During secondary drying the temperature is 
increased, maintaining the high vacuum conditions, to 
promote this desorption process.
The lyophilization process has been studied from both 
an economic (Stratta et  al. 2020) and an environmental 
sustainability (Gamiz 2019) perspective. These stud -
ies concluded that primary drying is the process phase 
involving the highest resource consumption, and that 
shortening primary drying would therefore have the 
greatest impact on economic efficiency and sustainability.
In this context, the use of mathematical models has 
emerged as a powerful tool to guide the selection of pri -
mary drying process conditions and therefore optimize 
efficiency (Tchessalov et  al. 2023). Several mechanistic 
models have emerged over the years (Karel 1975, Pikal 
et  al. 1984, Pikal 1985, Liapis and Bruttini 1995, Trelea 
et  al. 2007, Pisano et  al. 2011a) and the application of 
such models in process scale-up and transfer has also 
been discussed (Trappler 2001, Jennings 2002, Tchessa -
lov et al. 2007, Tchessalov et al. 2017).
The main aim of these models is the identification of 
primary drying conditions, in particular chamber pres -
sure and shelf temperature, that minimize cycle times, 
while still resulting in acceptable appearance of the final 
product (Patel et al. 2017). For this purpose, the models 
aim at calculating three crucial attributes of the primary 
drying process, i.e., i) the product temperature, ii) the 
water vapor mass flux during primary drying, and iii) the 
time required to complete the sublimation process.
These three attributes are related to three correspond -
ing failure modes of the primary drying phase. First, the 
sublimation interface temperature should remain below 
a formulation specific critical value (known as collapse 
temperature for amorphous materials or melting tem -
perature for crystalline ones) in order to preserve the 
integrity of the porous structure within the dried product 
(Pikal and Shah 1990).
Second, the water vapor mass flux during sublimation 
is constrained by the design of the freeze-dryer. The water 
vapor that is removed from the vials is conveyed to the 
condenser, where desublimation occurs. The water vapor 
molecules accelerate in presence of restrictions along the 
path to the condenser, but their velocity cannot exceed

the speed of sound. When this condition is reached, the 
flow is said to be choked, and any additional increase in 
water vapor flow rate results in an uncontrolled increase 
in chamber pressure (Searles 2004, Patel et al. 2010a). A 
similar loss in chamber pressure control is encountered 
when the water vapor flow rate exceeds the maximum 
deposition capacity of the condenser (ice accumulation 
capacity as defined in (Ganguly and Alexeenko 2012)), 
resulting in an uncontrolled increase of the condenser 
temperature. Both phenomena represent limits of the 
freeze-drying equipment.
Lastly, the primary drying time should be long enough 
to ensure that sublimation is complete within the whole 
batch before switching to secondary drying. If sublima -
tion is not complete before the start of the ramp to the 
secondary drying phase, the so-called meltback occurs. 
During meltback, residual ice will melt and dissolve parts 
of the established dried product. This translates into poor 
product appearance and higher moisture levels (FDA 
2014).
Besides helping to avoid these undesired failure modes 
of primary drying, modelling can also support regulatory 
submissions. The lack of a scientific rationale behind the 
selection of lyophilization cycle conditions is one of the 
most commonly cited manufacturing-related deficiencies 
identified in new drug applications for lyophilized inject -
able products (FDA 2014, Rhieu et al. 2020). Mathemati -
cal models can provide the necessary understanding to 
substantiate the selection of the process conditions.
At last, models are an integral part of the Quality-by-
Design (QbD) framework (Patel and Pikal 2013, Yu et al. 
2014). They support in process understanding by quan -
tifying the relationship between critical process param -
eters (CPPs), critical material attributes (CMAs), and 
critical quality attributes (CQAs) of the drug product. 
For instance, models can support building a design space 
(DSp). The DSp is a graphical representation of the CPPs 
settings that result in acceptable CQAs, with the inclu -
sion of boundaries defined by formulation properties 
and equipment limitations. It is especially useful when 
interaction effects exist between different CPPs, and the 
acceptable range of one CPP depends on the setting of 
another CPP or CMA.
Several DSp’s for lyophilization have been described in 
the literature (Chang and Fischer 1995, Nail and Searles 
2008, Fissore et al. 2011, Scutella and Bourles 2020, Srini-
vasan et al. 2023), but these DSp’s have not always been 
demonstrated to provide assurance of quality. Assurance 
of quality is instead needed to define a DSp in the regula -
tory sense (ICH 2009). When a DSp includes risk assess -
ment and is experimentally verified it can provide that 
quality assurance. Moreover, a change in normal oper -
ating ranges (NORs) within the DSp is not considered 
as a change to the marketing authorization dossier and 
can be managed without regulatory action (ICH 2009, 
EMA 2013). In the context of this manuscript we con -
sider that the presented DSp is regulatory compliant. A 
closely related idea to the DSp concept is the notion of 
proven acceptable ranges (PARs). A PAR is defined as a 
characterized range of a CPP for which operation within 
this range, while keeping other CPPs constant, will result 
in acceptable CQAs (ICH 2009). Also in this case, the 
advantage of identifying PARs is that a change in pro -
cess conditions within the approved PARs can be man -
aged without regulatory action (EMA 2017). Whenever 
no DSp or PARs are available, only the target operating 
conditions, and the corresponding NOR presented in the 
marketing authorization dossier are allowed. The NOR 
describes the region around the target operating condi -
tions that contain common operational variability. The 
NOR, in contrast to PAR, is not intended to introduce 
flexibility in the conditions for manufacturing (EMA 
2017).
Mechanistic primary drying models have already 
proven to be extremely useful in identifying optimal 
CPPs, supporting scale up and process transfer as well as 
building the DSp. However, a weakness of current models 
is the ability to robustly deal with process variability. Pri -
mary drying is intrinsically affected by variability (Pikal 
et al. 2018). As an example, the ice crystals formed during 
freezing turn into the pores of the dried layer during pri -
mary drying. The size and interconnectivity of such pores 
determines the resistance offered by the dried layer to the 
removal of water vapor from the sublimation front. Such 
resistance, generally indicated as Rp in most primary dry -
ing models, therefore shows variability within the batch, 
as freezing is a stochastic process, and the ice crystal size 
and shape varies between different vials (Arsiccio et  al. 
2017, Nuytten et al. 2021).
Similarly, the heat transfer towards different vials, 
quantified via the heat transfer coefficient K v, is also nei -
ther uniformly nor normally distributed within the batch, 
due to the different contribution of radiation in edge ver -
sus central vials (Ehlers et al. 2021, Fontana et al. 2023). 
The size of the vials and the filling volume also show a 
distribution due to variabilities of the manufacturing and 
filling process. Finally, the CPPs such as shelf tempera -
ture and chamber pressure are characterized by temporal 
and spatial variability within their NOR, which are dic -
tated by the controller and the design of the freeze-dryer.
The authors already discussed how to take this vari -
ability into consideration in a primary drying model by 
applying a Monte Carlo uncertainty analysis (Vanbillem -
ont et al. 2023). In this work we further extend this statis-
tical approach and show how it can be incorporated into 
the calculation of the DSp. We suggest a new procedure 
Page 4 of 20Vanbillemont et al. AAPS Open           (2025) 11:13 
to accurately estimate the variability of the resistance to 
mass transfer (Rp) while iteratively building and verifying 
the DSp. Our design space approach for primary drying, 
contrary to previously published ones, includes an uncer-
tainty analysis to consider process variability, therefore 
showing the probability of failure (PoF) for each com -
bination of the CPPs. This allows a robust evaluation of 
PARs or the DSp, in line with regulatory requirements. 
We prove how this DSp, including uncertainty analysis, 
can be used to guide both cycle optimization and process 
transfer. In addition, we show how to precisely perform 
verification and validation (V&V) activities for the devel -
oped primary drying model to enhance model credibility, 
in accordance with the ASME V&V 40–2018 guideline 
(ASME 2018).
Materials and methods
Mechanistic model structure
A mechanistic model of the primary drying step of a 
pharmaceutical lyophilization process on the vial level 
was based on a heat-mass balance coded in Python 
v3.10.6. The governing equations (Eqs.  1 till 7) were 
inspired by the work of Pikal (Pikal et  al. 1984). Equa -
tion 1 is typically called a Kv-Rp model in literature (Leys 
et  al. 2020, Tchessalov et  al. 2021, Vanbillemont et  al. 
2020a) since it employs a heat transfer coefficient ( Kv) to 
describe the heat interaction between the vial and freeze-
dryer and a resistance to mass transfer ( Rp) to explain the 
flow restriction of the newly formed dried layer towards 
the escaping vapor flow.

solved to get an estimate of the sublimation interface 
temperature ( Ti) and temperature difference across the 
frozen layer (ΔT) in function of CPP’s and CMA’s. CPP’s 
included the shelf temperature ( Ts) and chamber pres -
sure (Pc) since they dictate the primary drying protocol.

total sublimation rate of the batch (∑msub) was calculated 
by multiplying the vial load (n vials) with the averaged 
simulated sublimation rate at sample level (m̄ sub). When 
a sample reached Dried sample status, it was excluded 
from the uncertainty analysis and only the remaining 
samples were advanced to the next integration step.
A risk assessment was conducted using a statistical 
sampling method on the outputs of the uncertainty anal -
ysis, estimating the risks of the three failure modes. The 
failure mode of collapse was defined as T i being equal or 
surpassing the critical temperature (T c). At each integra -
tion step the percentage of samples with collapse was 
determined (i.e., p(collapse)). Moreover, when a sample 
reached collapse status it retained it for the remainder 
of the analysis. Next, the failure mode of meltback was 
defined as samples still containing a frozen product layer 
at end of primary drying and could be estimated by the 
percentage of samples still inside the uncertainty analysis 
(i.e., p(meltback)). At last, the failure mode of equipment 
limits (i.e., p(equipment)) was determined by comparing 
the ∑msub with sublimation rates sustained under mini -
mal controllable chamber pressures (m sub_max) inside the 
variability of the NOR.
Primary drying dynamics were simulated using the 
uncertainty analysis methodology on an operational pro -
cess space. The operational process space was described 
by a predetermined array with size 10 × 10 of primary 
drying protocols defined by target T s and P c. For each 
of these 100 primary drying protocols the risks of the 
three failure modes (i.e., p(collapse), p(meltback) and 
p(equipment)) were determined. A log-linear surface 
response model was fitted to each of the three risks 
matrices in function of the target T s and P c. These risks 
were subsequently interpolated to higher resolution 
operational process space matrices of primary drying 
protocols with a T s resolution of 1  °C and P c resolution 
of 1 Pa. The total risk of failure p(failure)  for the proto -
cols was subsequently computed by taking the union 
of the three interpolated risks. At last, the process 
robustness space was constructed by intersecting the 
p(failure) matrix with the accepted probability of failure 
(PoF). A PoF of 0.1% was assumed in this work, mean -
ing 1 vial in 1,000 was accepted to display a failure mode. 
The area within the process robustness space where 
p(failure) < PoF was colored in shades of blue and repre -
sented the primary drying protocols that were defined as 
robust and thus constitutes a DSp.
The optimal protocol at NOR was selected by repeated 
binary erosion of the DSp matrix till an isolated proto -
col was obtained. When multiple isolated protocols were 
obtained, the protocol with lowest p(failure)  was chosen. 
This approach guaranteed the selection of the NOR with 
largest associated PARs. The PARs were defined as the 
univariate ranges of T s and Pc that extend from the NOR 
till the edges of the DSp.
Dynamic visualizations
A dynamic dashboard was created in TIBCO Spotfire 
Analyst 12.0.3 (California, USA) to import and process 
the raw process data from the freeze-dryer. Data process-
ing included the necessary transformations and slicing 
of the data into the different freeze-drying phases such 
as freezing, primary drying and secondary drying. The 
mechanistic primary drying model, risk assessment and 
Rp extraction were programmed in Python as data func -
tions within the dashboard to allow easy interaction with 
the dataset. The primary drying process data was used 
to estimate the R p parameters. The dashboard allowed 
Rp regression from multiple lyophilization cycles of the 
same product to get a more accurate estimation of the R p 
profile and its variability.
Model credibility
To enhance the trust in the predictive capabilities of the 
mechanistic model and risk assessment methodology as 
described above, several model verification and valida -
tion (V&V) activities were performed. These V&V activi -
ties were based on the ASME V&V 40–2018 guidelines 
(ASME 2018). Despite the fact that the application field 
of this guideline is medical devices, it is the opinion of 
the authors that the methodology described in this guide-
line can be applied to pharmaceutical products as well, to 
enhance model credibility.
The proposed risk assessment methodology can be 
applied to support several drug product development 
activities for lyophilized products. Experimental valida -
tion of the first three use cases as outlined below are dis -
cussed within this work.
Envisioned use cases of the methodology
• Optimization of the primary drying process with the 
main aim to reduce process time
• Assessment of process robustness
• Support in scale-up or process transfer between lyo -
philizers
• Support in root-cause analysis of primary drying pro-
cess failures

Code and calculation verification
Code verification and calculation verification activi -
ties were performed on the final version of the dynamic 
dashboard including all data functions. Code verifica -
tion involved inventorying and assessing the quality of all 
software packages, interpreters and Python libraries that 
were applied. Next, a second operator check was per -
formed on all in-house built code to remove errors in the 
source code and increase readability.
In a next step, calculation verification was performed 
on the risk assessment methodology. The impact of two 
numerical parameters were evaluated on the accuracy 
of the results: the integration timestep (dt) and num -
ber of samples (n samples). For this purpose, the same case 
study as described in section 3.3.5 was used for a single 
primary drying protocol at P c 8 Pa and T s −24 °C. The 
default dt was defined as 1/500 th of the primary drying 
time predicted using nominal parameter settings. This 
ensures that the primary drying dynamics are covered by 
at least 500 equidistant datapoints. To achieve a statisti -
cally relevant number of samples the default n samples was 
fixed at 4,096. The analytical truth was calculated using 
1/25 th of the default dt and 16-fold the default n samples. 
Next, to investigate the model sensitivity towards dt, 
a series of predictions were performed between 1/25 
th and 25-fold of the default dt while keeping n samples at 
the default value. Similarly, predictions were made using 
16-fold till 1/16 th of the default nsamples while keeping the 
default dt. The error in comparison to the analytical truth 
was calculated for the three main outcomes of the model 
(i.e., maximum T i, primary drying time and equipment 
limits) to evaluate the ideal setting of nsamples and dt.

Materials
Model validation experiments were conducted using a 
biopharmaceutical model formulation comprising of 10 
mM Tris (Emprove Expert, Merck) at pH 7.3, 225 mM 
sucrose (Emprove Expert, Merck), 25 mg/ml BSA (≥ 
98%, Carl Roth) and 0.01% polysorbate 20 (PS20) (Super

Two freeze-dryers from Martin Christ (Osterode, Ger -

were performed at the scale-1 pilot freeze-dryer, while

Model input parameter variability estimation
Variation from the target CPPs can be expected due to 
the use of process controllers in the freeze-dryer. Moreo -
ver, a distribution of pressure across the chamber and a 
temperature distribution across the shelves are reported 
in literature (Strongrich and Alexeenko 2021, Rasetto 
et  al. 2010). To account for these variations, a uniform 
distribution with a limit of ± 1 °C for T s (T s_lim) and ± 1 
Pa ( Pc_lim) for Pc was chosen. The NORs were therefore 
defined as the target primary drying protocol enlarged by 
the Ts_lim and Pc_lim.
A complete shelf at scale-1 of 6R vials (i.e., 257 vials) 
was characterized for their inner (d i) and outer diam -
eters (d o) to determine the averages (d̄ I and d̄ o) and 
standard deviations (σ di and σ do), assuming normal dis -
tributions (see Fig.  1). Next, these vials were filled with 
2.5 ml (approximately 8 mm liquid filling height) of water 
for the K v determination. The filling volume accuracy by 
means of the standard deviation (σ Vfill) was determined 
gravimetrically on these vials.

Constraints estimation
Each of the three failure modes was defined by constrain-
ing parameters which limited the operational process 
space to achieve the risk assessment. The critical temper -
ature of the formulation (T c) influenced the p(collapse). 
The Tc was determined using a FDSC 196 freeze-drying 
microscopy (FDM) (Linkam, Surray, UK) with 10 × mag -
nification applying a cooling ramp of 10 °C/min to −50 
°C, followed by a heating ramp of 1 °C/min at 10 Pa. Next, 
the p(meltback) was determined by setting a predefined 
target primary drying time (PD time).
At last, to determine the p(equipment) the maximal 
sublimation rate (m sub_max) was measured under either 
choked flow or condenser deposition capacity conditions. 
Minimal controllable pressure experiments inspired by 
Srinivasan et  al. (Srinivasan et  al. 2021) were executed. 
Bottomless trays were lined with thin plastic sheets and 
filled with water to form ice slabs upon freezing. Each 
tray contained approximately 1.2 L (about 1  cm fill 
height) of deionized water. Each tray was equipped with 
a product temperature sensor and three short lyophili -
zation cycles at a controlled Pc of 5, 28 and 50 Pa were 
executed where the loss-in-weight of the ice slabs were 
determined. The K v of this system was computed using 
Eq. 8 and regressed in function of P c according to Eq.  9. 
Next, several lyophilization runs were executed with the 
ice slabs, where the target Pc was set to 0.1 Pa, which was 
well below the minimal controllable pressure of the lyo -
philizer. During these runs the Ts was varied between−60 
°C and 40 °C in steps of 10 °C, with a holding time of 2 h 
at each T s. The resulting P c at the end of the 2 h holding 
steps was considered as the minimal controllable pres -
sure and the maximal sublimation rate (m sub_max) at that 
point was estimated using the K v of the ice slab system. 
The Pc dependent msub_max datapoints were considered to 
be limited by a choked flow regime. A linear regression 
was made though these m sub_max datapoints to estimate 
the intercept (a CF) and slope (b CF) in function of P c. The 
Pc independent m sub_max datapoints were attributed to 
a condenser  deposition capacity limit and were used to 
obtain msub_Cond.
DSp building and verification
The dried product layer resistance (R p) versus dried layer 
length (L d) profile is dependent on the formulation and

A conservative freeze-drying process was first executed 
to have an initial estimate of the R p parameters describ -

freezing step of this conservative process comprised a 
cooling ramp of 1 °C/min till −45 °C, followed by a hold -
ing step of 2  h at −45 °C. Next, primary drying started 
with a vacuum ramp till 10 Pa within 30 min at −45 °C. 
Subsequently, a ramp of 1  °C/min till −28 °C was pro -
grammed and held for 67 h. At last, secondary drying 
included a slow ramp of 0.12 °C/min till 30 °C, followed 
by a holding step at 30 °C for 8  h at the same chamber 
pressure setting as primary drying.
A risk assessment was performed applying the pre -

Hence forward an iterative approach was conducted to 
determine and verify the edges of robustness. The out -

the primary drying protocols associated with an edge of 
robustness, i.e., at the intersection point between two 
different failure modes. Next, that specific primary dry -

parameters were updated by regression on the aggre -

the updated R p parameters to estimate the next edge of 
robustness. The first edge protocol that was explored was 
at the intersection of collapse and equipment limits (i.e.,

of collapse and meltback was investigated. At last, the

Process transfer
The main aim of the process transfer was to keep the pri -
mary drying kinetics, i.e., the thermal history, as similar 
as possible between both scales (Tchessalov et al. 2017). 
The rationale was to adapt CPPs to anticipate the scale 
dependent CMAs changes in order to maintain the 
CQAs. The scale dependent CMAs changes in a primary 
drying process are concentrated in the heat transfer coef-
ficient (Kv) and equipment limits constraints. Therefore, 
the AKv, BKv, CKv, RSDKv, nvials, aCF, bCF and m sub_Cond of 
the scale-2 freeze-dryer were applied in the calculations. 
Next, the risk assessment was repeated at scale-2, apply -
ing the final R p parameters from scale-1. The optimal 
protocol at NOR for the freeze-dryer scale-2 was chosen 
as the point within the risk assessment matrix (i.e., the 
unverified DSp) with comparable primary drying kinet -
ics as scale-1. This optimal primary drying protocol was 
finally validated experimentally on the scale-2 freeze-
dryer. Since the lower Pc process space in the risk assess -
ment was not limited by choked flow limits but rather 
due to the model calibration limits (i.e., 5 till 25 Pa), only 
one full shelf of the scale-2 freeze-dryer was loaded in 
this validation experiment.
Morphological quality attributes
The morphological appearance of the lyophilized cakes 
was evaluated according to an image-based method, 
designed to identify cake shrinkage, meltback or col -
lapse (Patel et al. 2017) and minimize operator bias. For 
this purpose, eight high-resolution pictures of the lyo -
philized vials were taken from the front side at each 45° 
rotation angle. The images were converted to grayscale 
and subsequently binarized using Otzu’s thresholding 
method (Otsu 1979). After binarization, the lyophilized 
cake was represented by white pixels, while the vial wall 
and background by black pixels. The fraction of white 
pixels in each image was then counted, therefore mak -
ing it possible to define a cake volume fraction, by aver -
aging over the eight collected pictures. Twenty-six vials 
were selected from different batch positions after each 
lyophilization run, and morphologically characterized 
using this approach, to obtain a distribution of cake vol -
ume fraction across the whole batch. Exemplary pictures 
of this methodology are provided in the supplementary 
information (Figure S1).
Residual water content
The residual water content of the freeze-dried products 
was determined by using the coulometric Karl Fischer 
titrator Aqua 40.00 (Analytik Jena GmbH), which is 
equipped with a headspace module. The sample prepa -
ration of the freeze-dried product was performed in a 
glovebox under controlled humidity (≤ 10% r.h.).
For the measurements, about 20–30 mg of freeze-dried 
product were weighed into 2R glass vials (Schott, Mainz, 
Germany) and heated to 120 °C in the oven connected to 
the reaction vessel via a tubing system. The evaporated 
water was transferred into the titration solution and the 
amount of water was determined. The measurement was 
performed until no more water evaporation was detect -
able (the baseline was determined by the drift before a 
measurement series, and the stop criteria by the oscil -
lation around the baseline). The water content of the 
sample was calculated by taking the environmental mois-
ture as determined in three blanks (i.e., empty vials pre -
pared in the measurement environment and analyzed by 
using the same method and settings as the sample) into 
consideration.
Results and discussion
Model context
According to the ASME V&V 40–2018 guidelines (ASME 
2018), the first step which is needed to assess the cred -
ibility of a model is a clear identification of the questions 
of interest (QOI) that the model is trying to answer, and 
of the corresponding context of use (COU). The follow -
ing QOIs were identified for the mechanistic model of 
primary drying discussed in the present work:
1. Finding optimal CPPs (i.e., combination of T s, Pc and 
PD time) therefore defining the NOR for the primary 
drying phase of lyophilization.
2. Proving robustness of the optimal CPPs by establish -
ing a DSp.
3. Assisting in transfer of a lyophilization primary dry -
ing protocol between different equipment.
In reference to the above-mentioned QOIs 1–3, the fol-
lowing COUs apply:
1. Mass and heat balance equations are applied to 
the primary drying phase of lyophilization, and an 
uncertainty analysis based on distribution of input 
variables and model parameters is also performed. 
A matrix of chamber pressure and shelf temperature 
Page 10 of 20Vanbillemont et al. AAPS Open           (2025) 11:13 
values is simulated with different process time limits 
and the probability of different failure modes (col -
lapse, meltback and exceeding the equipment lim -
its) is computed for each element of the matrix. This 
allows the selection of optimal CPPs (QOI #1). The 
model can further be used to transfer a lyophilization 
primary drying protocol to a different scale (scale-
2) by measuring scale-dependent model parameters 
(heat transfer coefficient and equipment limits) at 
scale-2, and by re-applying the model (QOI #3). A 
robustness assessment (QOI #2) is performed and 
the area of robustness surrounding the optimal CPPs 
is validated experimentally. This results in the genera-
tion of a DSp, i.e., a representation allowing the iden-
tification of a robustness area around the selected 
CPPs. In this case, any change in CPPs within the 
verified DSp can be managed without regulatory 
action.
2. The same procedure for the identification of the opti-
mal CPPs at scale 1 and/or 2 is applied, as discussed 
in COU #1. However, process robustness (QOI #2) 
is not fully assessed at commercial scale, and only 
the optimal cycle is validated experimentally. In this 
case, the model generates an unverified DSp, and 
the only accepted variability for the optimal CPPs is 
around their NORs. This is sufficient for submission 
of an application (EMA 2013), but an additional post-
approval DSp verification effort should be performed 
if the operational flexibility of a DSp approach is 
desired.
The next step for V&V activities, according to the 
ASME V&V 40–2018 guidelines, is the model risk classi -
fication. When the COU is clear, the risk associated with 
the application of the model can be assessed based on the 
model influence and decision consequence, according to 
the classification scheme shown in Table 3.
For both COUs discussed in this work, the model influ-
ence is classified as medium, as the model output is used 
to guide decisions on CPPs, but experimental verifica -
tion is used to confirm the model output and drive the 
final decision. Furthermore, the decision consequence 
is ranked as low, because appropriate experimental 
responses (i.e., the CQAs) are selected in the verification 
experiment to capture potential adverse effects in qual -
ity, safety or efficacy of the drug product (DP). Moreover, 
the appropriate experimental responses are determined 
across the whole regulatory design space at scale-1 (COU 
#1) and at the NOR at scale-2 (COU #2).

Code and calculation verification
A code and calculation verification on the final version 
of the dynamic dashboard, including all data functions, 
was subsequently performed. The model used the built-
in IronPython (v. 2.7) and Python (v. 3.10.6) interpreters 
in TIBCO Spotfire Analyst 12.0.3. The Python packages 
used included distfit 1.7.3, joblib 1.3.2, numpy 1.24.1, 
olefile 0.47, pandas 1.5.2, scikit-learn 1.4.0, scipy 1.12.0, 
statsmodels 0.14.1 and xlrd 2.0.1. TIBCO Spotfire and 
Python are established software, and no additional soft -
ware quality assurance was performed as part of the cur -
rent code and calculation verification.
As discussed in the Materials and Methods section, an 
analysis of the effect of the timestep dt and the number of 
samples nsamples on the accuracy of the model predictions 
was performed. The result of this investigation is shown 
in Fig. 3. From Fig. 3 it is evident that n samples has a much 
lower impact on the accuracy than dt (left vs. right panel 
of Fig. 3). Changing nsamples in the range 16x (i.e., 65,536) 
till 1/8x (i.e., 512) resulted in small error values (< 1%) on 
all model outputs, and only at the most extreme condi -
tion tested (1/16x, nsamples: 256) the error increases above 
1% as illustrated in Fig. 3.
To the contrary, increasing dt resulted in a significant 
decrease in accuracy, with errors up to 18% on the pre -
dicted equipment limits when dt is increased to 5 × the 
default (i.e., 1,320 s).
The analysis performed here also suggests that the 
default dt and n samples result in acceptably small errors 
(< 1%) compared to the reference (1/25 × dt, 16 × nsam-
ples) simulation. It should also be considered that the 
modelling approach used for the process robustness 
assessment involves random sampling of the n samples 
input vectors, meaning that some variability of the out -
put data is to be expected. The output variability due 
to the random sampling procedure was quantified by 
repeating the same calculation 6 times using the default 
timestep dt and number of samples n samples. As a result 
of this analysis, relative standard deviations of 0.08%, 
0.42% and 0.19% were obtained on the predicted maxi -
mum temperature, primary drying time and equipment 
capability limits, respectively. Based on this analysis, the 
variability on model outputs due to statistical sampling 
is of the same order of magnitude as the error between 
the default (1 × dt, 1 × nsamples) and the reference (1/25 
× dt, 16 × nsamples) simulation, further suggesting that the 
default solver parameters are adequate to ensure accu -
racy. Interestingly, the highest relative standard deviation 
was observed on the predicted primary drying time. This 
is not surprising since primary drying time predictions 
suffer from the error propagation in the estimation of the 
dried layer length. Meltback predictions are based on pri-
mary drying time estimations, and therefore the highest 
uncertainty on the design space calculation is expected to 
occur on the edge of failure due to meltback.

condenser coils below the stoppering plate. The choked-

Optimal cycle
After the iterative verification of the DSp, optimal cycle 
conditions were selected within the DSp and as far as 
possible from the boundaries of failure. Such conditions 
corresponded to T s of −24 °C, P c of 8 Pa and PD time of 
50 h. These conditions were defined as the NORs since 
they included the expected variability of the CPPs (T s ± 
1  °C and P c ± 1  Pa) as outlined in section  2.6.2. Process 
readouts from the experimental verification of this target 
operating condition (Fig. 9A) confirmed that the product 
temperature was consistently below the collapse limit, 
and that the sublimation phase was completed before 
the start of the ramp to secondary drying, as indicated by 
comparative pressure measurements. Furthermore, the 
optimized process resulted in a 26% reduction in primary 
drying time compared to the conservative run performed 
for Rp extraction (from 68 to 50 h primary drying time).

Lyophilization process transfer
The risk assessment as shown in Fig.  10B was calculated 
by applying the scale-2 appropriate K v and equipment 
limit parameters, with the final R p  coefficients. Upon 
comparing the risk assessments at the two scales, it can 
be observed that the robust blue area shifted by 2 °C on 
the Ts-axis, and that this area at scale-2 was also slightly 
enlarged. This shift originated from a slightly worse heat 
transfer (lower K v regression in Fig.  4A) in the scale-2 
versus scale-1 freeze-dryer. To attain similar primary dry-
ing kinetics of the optimal protocols between both scales, 
the model predicted to adapt the scale-2 CPPs by T s + 
2 °C to sustain the heat flux. The enlargement of the pre -
dicted robustness area was driven by the RSD Kv (scale-1: 
15.1% vs. scale-2: 11.0%). The larger shelf area at scale-2 
versus scale-1 resulted in relatively less atypical and out -
lying vials of groups 1–4 versus the more homogene -
ous groups 5–6. Furthermore, the scale-1 freeze-dryer 
displayed a plexiglass door enhancing radiation effects 
which were less present at scale-2 due to the presence 
of a stainless-steel door. Both effects contributed to the 
decrease in RSDKv at scale-2 and therefore to the enlarge-
ment of the predicted robustness area. In other words, a 
higher homogeneity between the samples is expected in 
scale-2 versus scale-1.

Conclusions
In the present work, a modelling framework to increase 
the robustness of process parameters selection for the 
primary drying phase of freeze-drying has been devel -
oped and discussed. The model performs an uncertainty 
analysis on the input parameters of a primary drying 
process and propagates such uncertainty to estimate the 
distribution of the CQAs. By doing this, the model can 
estimate the risk associated with different failure modes, 
such as collapse, meltback or exceeding the equipment 
limits. The possibility of using the model for transferring 
process parameters between different equipment, e.g., 
during process scale-up, has also been discussed.
We have further shown how V&V activities can be 
performed to increase model credibility. Our model vali -
dation experiments, performed on a representative biop -
harmaceutical formulation, confirmed that the model 
can robustly predict the combination of CPPs leading to 
acceptable CQAs. This leads to the generation of a risk 
assessment matrix, i.e., a visual representation of the 
impact of CPPs on CQAs. When experimentally verified, 
the risk assessment matrix becomes a regulatory com -
pliant DSp. The advantage of a DSp approach is that it 
introduces flexibility in the conditions for manufacturing, 
as any change in CPPs within the DSp can be managed 
without regulatory action.
Overall, we think that the primary drying model herein 
discussed represents an advancement in the execution of 
uncertainty analysis for process robustness estimation. 
Furthermore, the framework for model V&V, and for 
DSp generation that we present streamlines the steps 
required to increase the credibility of a model, and to 
adequately include such information in a marketing 
authorization dossier. Such framework could be extended 
to different models and different unit operations, as well, 
as the overall approach and nomenclature we use are 
generally applicable, and not only limited to the field of 
freeze-drying.
