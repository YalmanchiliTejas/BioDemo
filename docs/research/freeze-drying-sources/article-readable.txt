Abstract
Lyophilization, or freeze-drying, is a widespread process for stabilizing (bio)pharmaceutical products. The primary drying phase is resource-intensive, making its optimization a focal point for improving economic and environmental sustainability. Mechanistic modeling of the primary drying phase has emerged as a key tool for designing robust processes, identifying optimal critical process parameters, and minimizing failure modes such as product collapse, uncontrolled pressure increase, or incomplete sublimation. This study extended the traditional modeling frameworks by incorporating an uncertainty analysis to account for process variability originating from process parameters and material attributes. Model validation and verification activities were performed to establish model credibility. The ensuing risk assessment quantified the failure mode probabilities. This methodology was capable of ensuring process robustness at a pilot scale freeze-dryer by construction of a regulatory compliant design space and the establishment of proven acceptable ranges. An iterative approach to collect process knowledge was conducted by updating the dried layer resistance estimates while verifying the design space. The transfer of the normal operating ranges to a commercial scale freeze-dryer was assisted by the model and was experimentally verified. Experimental results confirmed the model’s capability to predict process parameter combinations yielding acceptable critical quality attributes while minimizing process time. The model provides a scientific rationale for process parameters selection and can support in the process optimization, robustness assessment and process transfer activities necessary for a regulatory submission.

Freeze-Drying Process Optimization in Pharmaceutical Applications
Introduction
Lyophilization, or freeze-drying, is commonly used to increase the shelf life of (bio)pharmaceuticals. During lyophilization, the product is first frozen, and the ice crystals are subsequently removed by sublimation during the primary drying phase. The removal of ice occurs at low temperature and high vacuum conditions during primary drying, making this process particularly suitable for heat-sensitive molecules. The desorption of the unfrozen water molecules takes place during secondary drying, which represents the subsequent phase of the freeze-drying process. During secondary drying the temperature is increased, maintaining the high vacuum conditions, to promote this desorption process.
The lyophilization process has been studied from both an economic (Stratta et al.
2020
) and an environmental sustainability (Gamiz
2019
) perspective. These studies concluded that primary drying is the process phase involving the highest resource consumption, and that shortening primary drying would therefore have the greatest impact on economic efficiency and sustainability.
In this context, the use of mathematical models has emerged as a powerful tool to guide the selection of primary drying process conditions and therefore optimize efficiency (Tchessalov et al.
2023
). Several mechanistic models have emerged over the years (Karel
1975
, Pikal et al.
1984
, Pikal
1985
, Liapis and Bruttini
1995
, Trelea et al.
2007
, Pisano et al.
2011a
) and the application of such models in process scale-up and transfer has also been discussed (Trappler
2001
, Jennings
2002
, Tchessalov et al.
2007
, Tchessalov et al.
2017
).
The main aim of these models is the identification of primary drying conditions, in particular chamber pressure and shelf temperature, that minimize cycle times, while still resulting in acceptable appearance of the final product (Patel et al.
2017
). For this purpose, the models aim at calculating three crucial attributes of the primary drying process, i.e., i) the product temperature, ii) the water vapor mass flux during primary drying, and iii) the time required to complete the sublimation process.
These three attributes are related to three corresponding failure modes of the primary drying phase. First, the sublimation interface temperature should remain below a formulation specific critical value (known as collapse temperature for amorphous materials or melting temperature for crystalline ones) in order to preserve the integrity of the porous structure within the dried product (Pikal and Shah
1990
).
Second, the water vapor mass flux during sublimation is constrained by the design of the freeze-dryer. The water vapor that is removed from the vials is conveyed to the condenser, where desublimation occurs. The water vapor molecules accelerate in presence of restrictions along the path to the condenser, but their velocity cannot exceed the speed of sound. When this condition is reached, the flow is said to be choked, and any additional increase in water vapor flow rate results in an uncontrolled increase in chamber pressure (Searles
2004
, Patel et al.
2010a
). A similar loss in chamber pressure control is encountered when the water vapor flow rate exceeds the maximum deposition capacity of the condenser (ice accumulation capacity as defined in (Ganguly and Alexeenko
2012
)), resulting in an uncontrolled increase of the condenser temperature. Both phenomena represent limits of the freeze-drying equipment.
Lastly, the primary drying time should be long enough to ensure that sublimation is complete within the whole batch before switching to secondary drying. If sublimation is not complete before the start of the ramp to the secondary drying phase, the so-called meltback occurs. During meltback, residual ice will melt and dissolve parts of the established dried product. This translates into poor product appearance and higher moisture levels (FDA
2014
).
Besides helping to avoid these undesired failure modes of primary drying, modelling can also support regulatory submissions. The lack of a scientific rationale behind the selection of lyophilization cycle conditions is one of the most commonly cited manufacturing-related deficiencies identified in new drug applications for lyophilized injectable products (FDA
2014
, Rhieu et al.
2020
). Mathematical models can provide the necessary understanding to substantiate the selection of the process conditions.
At last, models are an integral part of the Quality-by-Design (QbD) framework (Patel and Pikal
2013
, Yu et al.
2014
). They support in process understanding by quantifying the relationship between critical process parameters (CPPs), critical material attributes (CMAs), and critical quality attributes (CQAs) of the drug product. For instance, models can support building a design space (DSp). The DSp is a graphical representation of the CPPs settings that result in acceptable CQAs, with the inclusion of boundaries defined by formulation properties and equipment limitations. It is especially useful when interaction effects exist between different CPPs, and the acceptable range of one CPP depends on the setting of another CPP or CMA.
Several DSp’s for lyophilization have been described in the literature (Chang and Fischer
1995
, Nail and Searles
2008
, Fissore et al.
2011
, Scutella and Bourles
2020
, Srinivasan et al.
2023
), but these DSp’s have not always been demonstrated to provide assurance of quality. Assurance of quality is instead needed to define a DSp in the regulatory sense (ICH
2009
). When a DSp includes risk assessment and is experimentally verified it can provide that quality assurance. Moreover, a change in normal operating ranges (NORs) within the DSp is not considered as a change to the marketing authorization dossier and can be managed without regulatory action (ICH
2009
, EMA
2013
). In the context of this manuscript we consider that the presented DSp is regulatory compliant. A closely related idea to the DSp concept is the notion of proven acceptable ranges (PARs). A PAR is defined as a characterized range of a CPP for which operation within this range, while keeping other CPPs constant, will result in acceptable CQAs (ICH
2009
). Also in this case, the advantage of identifying PARs is that a change in process conditions within the approved PARs can be managed without regulatory action (EMA
2017
). Whenever no DSp or PARs are available, only the target operating conditions, and the corresponding NOR presented in the marketing authorization dossier are allowed. The NOR describes the region around the target operating conditions that contain common operational variability. The NOR, in contrast to PAR, is not intended to introduce flexibility in the conditions for manufacturing (EMA
2017
).
Mechanistic primary drying models have already proven to be extremely useful in identifying optimal CPPs, supporting scale up and process transfer as well as building the DSp. However, a weakness of current models is the ability to robustly deal with process variability. Primary drying is intrinsically affected by variability (Pikal et al.
2018
). As an example, the ice crystals formed during freezing turn into the pores of the dried layer during primary drying. The size and interconnectivity of such pores determines the resistance offered by the dried layer to the removal of water vapor from the sublimation front. Such resistance, generally indicated as
R
p
in most primary drying models, therefore shows variability within the batch, as freezing is a stochastic process, and the ice crystal size and shape varies between different vials (Arsiccio et al.
2017
, Nuytten et al.
2021
).
Similarly, the heat transfer towards different vials, quantified via the heat transfer coefficient
K
v
, is also neither uniformly nor normally distributed within the batch, due to the different contribution of radiation in edge versus central vials (Ehlers et al.
2021
, Fontana et al.
2023
). The size of the vials and the filling volume also show a distribution due to variabilities of the manufacturing and filling process. Finally, the CPPs such as shelf temperature and chamber pressure are characterized by temporal and spatial variability within their NOR, which are dictated by the controller and the design of the freeze-dryer.
The authors already discussed how to take this variability into consideration in a primary drying model by applying a Monte Carlo uncertainty analysis (Vanbillemont et al.
2023
). In this work we further extend this statistical approach and show how it can be incorporated into the calculation of the DSp. We suggest a new procedure to accurately estimate the variability of the resistance to mass transfer (
R
p
)
while iteratively building and verifying the DSp. Our design space approach for primary drying, contrary to previously published ones, includes an uncertainty analysis to consider process variability, therefore showing the probability of failure (PoF) for each combination of the CPPs. This allows a robust evaluation of PARs or the DSp, in line with regulatory requirements. We prove how this DSp, including uncertainty analysis, can be used to guide both cycle optimization and process transfer. In addition, we show how to precisely perform verification and validation (V&V) activities for the developed primary drying model to enhance model credibility, in accordance with the ASME V&V 40–2018 guideline (ASME
2018
).

Dynamic visualizations
A dynamic dashboard was created in TIBCO Spotfire Analyst 12.0.3 (California, USA) to import and process the raw process data from the freeze-dryer. Data processing included the necessary transformations and slicing of the data into the different freeze-drying phases such as freezing, primary drying and secondary drying. The mechanistic primary drying model, risk assessment and

Model credibility
To enhance the trust in the predictive capabilities of the mechanistic model and risk assessment methodology as described above, several model verification and validation (V&V) activities were performed. These V&V activities were based on the ASME V&V 40–2018 guidelines (ASME
2018
). Despite the fact that the application field of this guideline is medical devices, it is the opinion of the authors that the methodology described in this guideline can be applied to pharmaceutical products as well, to enhance model credibility.
The proposed risk assessment methodology can be applied to support several drug product development activities for lyophilized products. Experimental validation of the first three use cases as outlined below are discussed within this work.
Envisioned use cases of the methodology
Optimization of the primary drying process with the main aim to reduce process time
Assessment of process robustness
Support in scale-up or process transfer between lyophilizers
Support in root-cause analysis of primary drying process failures
Code and calculation verification
Code verification and calculation verification activities were performed on the final version of the dynamic dashboard including all data functions. Code verification involved inventorying and assessing the quality of all software packages, interpreters and Python libraries that were applied. Next, a second operator check was performed on all in-house built code to remove errors in the source code and increase readability.

Morphological quality attributes
The morphological appearance of the lyophilized cakes was evaluated according to an image-based method, designed to identify cake shrinkage, meltback or collapse (Patel et al.
2017
) and minimize operator bias. For this purpose, eight high-resolution pictures of the lyophilized vials were taken from the front side at each 45° rotation angle. The images were converted to grayscale and subsequently binarized using Otzu’s thresholding method (Otsu
1979
). After binarization, the lyophilized cake was represented by white pixels, while the vial wall and background by black pixels. The fraction of white pixels in each image was then counted, therefore making it possible to define a cake volume fraction, by averaging over the eight collected pictures. Twenty-six vials were selected from different batch positions after each lyophilization run, and morphologically characterized using this approach, to obtain a distribution of cake volume fraction across the whole batch. Exemplary pictures of this methodology are provided in the supplementary information (Figure S1).
Residual water content
The residual water content of the freeze-dried products was determined by using the coulometric Karl Fischer titrator Aqua 40.00 (Analytik Jena GmbH), which is equipped with a headspace module. The sample preparation of the freeze-dried product was performed in a glovebox under controlled humidity (≤ 10% r.h.).
For the measurements, about 20–30 mg of freeze-dried product were weighed into 2R glass vials (Schott, Mainz, Germany) and heated to 120 °C in the oven connected to the reaction vessel via a tubing system. The evaporated water was transferred into the titration solution and the amount of water was determined. The measurement was performed until no more water evaporation was detectable (the baseline was determined by the drift before a measurement series, and the stop criteria by the oscillation around the baseline). The water content of the sample was calculated by taking the environmental moisture as determined in three blanks (i.e., empty vials prepared in the measurement environment and analyzed by using the same method and settings as the sample) into consideration.
Results and discussion
Model context
According to the ASME V&V 40–2018 guidelines (ASME
2018
), the first step which is needed to assess the credibility of a model is a clear identification of the questions of interest (QOI) that the model is trying to answer, and of the corresponding context of use (COU). The following QOIs were identified for the mechanistic model of primary drying discussed in the present work:
1.
Finding optimal CPPs (i.e., combination of
T
s
,
P
c
and
PD time
) therefore defining the NOR for the primary drying phase of lyophilization.
2.
Proving robustness of the optimal CPPs by establishing a DSp.
3.
Assisting in transfer of a lyophilization primary drying protocol between different equipment.
In reference to the above-mentioned QOIs 1–3, the following COUs apply:
1.
Mass and heat balance equations are applied to the primary drying phase of lyophilization, and an uncertainty analysis based on distribution of input variables and model parameters is also performed. A matrix of chamber pressure and shelf temperature values is simulated with different process time limits and the probability of different failure modes (collapse, meltback and exceeding the equipment limits) is computed for each element of the matrix. This allows the selection of optimal CPPs (QOI #1). The model can further be used to transfer a lyophilization primary drying protocol to a different scale (scale-2) by measuring scale-dependent model parameters (heat transfer coefficient and equipment limits) at scale-2, and by re-applying the model (QOI #3). A robustness assessment (QOI #2) is performed and the area of robustness surrounding the optimal CPPs is validated experimentally. This results in the generation of a DSp, i.e., a representation allowing the identification of a robustness area around the selected CPPs. In this case, any change in CPPs within the verified DSp can be managed without regulatory action.
2.
The same procedure for the identification of the optimal CPPs at scale 1 and/or 2 is applied, as discussed in COU #1. However, process robustness (QOI #2) is not fully assessed at commercial scale, and only the optimal cycle is validated experimentally. In this case, the model generates an unverified DSp, and the only accepted variability for the optimal CPPs is around their NORs. This is sufficient for submission of an application (EMA
2013
), but an additional post-approval DSp verification effort should be performed if the operational flexibility of a DSp approach is desired.
The next step for V&V activities, according to the ASME V&V 40–2018 guidelines, is the model risk classification. When the COU is clear, the risk associated with the application of the model can be assessed based on the model influence and decision consequence, according to the classification scheme shown in Table
3
.

Lyophilization cycles for DSp verification
As outlined in the Materials and Methods section, an iterative approach was conducted to determine and verify the edges of robustness. At first, the edge of robustness located at the intersection of collapse and equipment limits was verified experimentally. At first, the temperature increases to reach a first plateau, where the endothermic process of sublimation is compensated by the heat transfer from the shelf. After this first plateau, a sharp change in slope is observed. This occurs when the sublimation front, in its movement from the top to the bottom of the product, reaches the sensing element of the temperature probe. The probe is not anymore located within the frozen area and measures the increase in temperature within the dried layer instead. When equilibrium is achieved between the different heat transfer modes towards the dried product, a second plateau is reached, which is typically close to or above the shelf temperature, due to radiation effects. All the product temperature readouts were below the

Conclusions
In the present work, a modelling framework to increase the robustness of process parameters selection for the primary drying phase of freeze-drying has been developed and discussed. The model performs an uncertainty analysis on the input parameters of a primary drying process and propagates such uncertainty to estimate the distribution of the CQAs. By doing this, the model can estimate the risk associated with different failure modes, such as collapse, meltback or exceeding the equipment limits. The possibility of using the model for transferring process parameters between different equipment, e.g., during process scale-up, has also been discussed.
We have further shown how V&V activities can be performed to increase model credibility. Our model validation experiments, performed on a representative biopharmaceutical formulation, confirmed that the model can robustly predict the combination of CPPs leading to acceptable CQAs. This leads to the generation of a risk assessment matrix, i.e., a visual representation of the impact of CPPs on CQAs. When experimentally verified, the risk assessment matrix becomes a regulatory compliant DSp. The advantage of a DSp approach is that it introduces flexibility in the conditions for manufacturing, as any change in CPPs within the DSp can be managed without regulatory action.
Overall, we think that the primary drying model herein discussed represents an advancement in the execution of uncertainty analysis for process robustness estimation. Furthermore, the framework for model V&V, and for DSp generation that we present streamlines the steps required to increase the credibility of a model, and to adequately include such information in a marketing authorization dossier. Such framework could be extended to different models and different unit operations, as well, as the overall approach and nomenclature we use are generally applicable, and not only limited to the field of freeze-drying.
